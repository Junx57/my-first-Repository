# Prescribed-Time Practical Tracking and Conditional Safety Analysis for Input-Constrained Two-Link Manipulators

## Abstract

This paper studies prescribed-time practical tracking for a two-link Euler–Lagrange manipulator subject to joint-torque limits, end-effector obstacle constraints, unknown state-dependent torque terms, and matched time-varying disturbances. The central difficulty is the compatibility of three input requirements: the nominal tracking controller dissipates the transformed tracking error, the high-order control barrier function (HOCBF) enforces obstacle safety, and the actuator box limits the admissible torque. A hard performance half-space can intersect the safety input set at an empty set even when a safe input exists. The proposed ITC-FP controller combines a bounded prescribed-time transformation, adaptive fuzzy compensation, a robust safety filter, and a quadratic program that keeps the HOCBF and actuator constraints hard while assigning a nonnegative slack variable to the performance row. The slack is normalized by the time-transformation scale and propagated through a comparison system. This produces an explicit performance-cost chain δp → rp → Dp → Δperf, where Δperf quantifies the tracking-error increase caused by safety coordination. The analysis establishes conditional end-effector safety, input satisfaction, pointwise soft-QP feasibility, and a prescribed-time practical tracking bound under model, feedback, regularity, and persistent safety-feasibility conditions. The numerical study uses a common two-link model and registered task protocols. In the 720-task budget matrix, ITC-FP completes 60/60 tasks in H1, H2, and H3, while the hard-performance and Full controllers complete 51/60, 0/60, and 60/60 tasks in the corresponding conditions. The independent LP audit covers 1340 MAT records and separates empty safety sets from hard performance-safety conflicts. The results support a conditional improvement in complete-task feasibility and quantify the associated tracking-performance cost.

**Keywords:** prescribed-time control; practical tracking; high-order control barrier function; input constraints; safety filter; adaptive fuzzy compensation; quadratic programming; feasibility analysis.

## 1. Introduction

### 1.1 Engineering background and research problem

Robot manipulators must track moving references while respecting actuator limits and maintaining a safe distance from obstacles. The requirements interact through the torque input: the tracking controller requests a nominal torque, the safety filter modifies that torque to enforce a barrier inequality, and the actuator box restricts the available correction. These requirements become more demanding when the manipulator contains unknown friction-like torques and time-varying disturbances. Classical adaptive robot control provides a foundation for compensating structured uncertainty [1], while operational-space obstacle avoidance established the importance of real-time geometric constraints for manipulators [25].

Prescribed-time control assigns the convergence deadline before the initial condition is known. For a practical tracking task, the objective is to enter a nonzero error tube by the prescribed deadline and retain the bound afterwards. This objective differs from finite-time convergence because the terminal tolerance and the deadline are fixed in advance. Prescribed-time feedback and dynamic high-gain constructions provide the relevant time-domain tools [2, 35, 36]. A bounded time transformation is required in an implementable controller because the ideal transformation becomes singular at the deadline.

Safety-critical control uses control barrier functions (CBFs) to encode forward-invariance requirements as online inequalities. CBF-QP methods combine a nominal controller with safety constraints [7], and high-order CBFs extend the construction to constraints whose input relative degree exceeds one [8]. Mechanical-system formulations, sampled-data safety, robust CBFs, and input-constrained safety filters have subsequently expanded the framework to robotic applications [9, 14, 15, 26, 29, 30]. The remaining issue is compatibility: the safe-input set may be nonempty while its intersection with a hard tracking-performance half-space is empty.

### 1.2 Related methods and research progress

The relevant literature can be organized into four lines. The first line develops adaptive and fuzzy compensation for uncertain nonlinear systems and robotic manipulators [1, 3–6]. These methods bound or approximate unknown dynamics, but the adaptive law alone does not encode obstacle safety or actuator feasibility.

The second line develops CBF and HOCBF safety filters. CBF-QPs provide a direct mechanism for combining a nominal input with safety constraints [7]. HOCBFs handle arbitrary relative degree and explicitly address feasibility issues caused by multiple safety constraints and limited actuation [8]. Robust CBFs incorporate uncertainty estimates [9, 17], while soft-minimum barriers, backup CBFs, embedded CBFs, and proxy CBFs address actuation limits, multiple constraints, and high-relative-degree design [10, 11, 16, 18]. Sampled-data formulations and whole-body manipulator controllers connect continuous-time safety conditions with digital execution [14, 15, 21, 30].

The third line studies prescribed-time tracking and prescribed performance. Prescribed-time regulation has been developed through time-varying feedback and dynamic high-gain designs [35, 36]. Prescribed-time tracking with guaranteed performance and prescribed-time control under measurement noise address the relation between deadline accuracy, performance envelopes, and implementation uncertainty [37, 38]. These results provide the time-domain framework used here.

The fourth line studies safety filters and feasibility. Predictive safety filters and probabilistic safety certification enforce safety for constrained nonlinear systems under uncertainty [33, 34]. Feasibility conditions for CBF-based optimal control and continuity of multiple-CBF feedback provide tools for diagnosing the intersection of safety constraints [27, 28]. Recent reviews emphasize that robustness, sampling, input limitations, and data-driven uncertainty must be treated as part of the safety argument [31, 32].

### 1.3 Research gap and focused question

Existing tracking and safety results leave a specific compatibility question. A safety filter changes the physical torque used by the manipulator, but a prescribed-time Lyapunov proof is usually written for the nominal torque. A hard performance row can then make the QP infeasible even when the safety and actuator constraints admit a solution. The resulting failure can be caused by an empty safe-input set or by a conflict introduced only by the performance row; these mechanisms have different interpretations and require separate diagnostics.

This paper asks: **How can the performance effect of safety filtering be incorporated into a prescribed-time tracking bound while preserving hard safety and actuator constraints, and how can hard performance-safety conflicts be quantified at the input level?**

### 1.4 Contributions

The paper makes four contributions.

1. It formulates a two-link manipulator problem with prescribed-time practical tracking, end-effector HOCBF safety, torque-box constraints, unknown matched torque terms, and time-varying matched disturbances.
2. It proposes ITC-FP, which combines a bounded time transformation, adaptive fuzzy compensation, and a QP that keeps safety and input constraints hard while relaxing only the performance row.
3. It defines the pointwise minimum performance slack and propagates the normalized slack through a two-level comparison system. The resulting Δperf term gives a computable tracking-performance cost for safety coordination.
4. It evaluates the mechanism with registered budget, holdout, external-baseline, front-obstacle, sensitivity, and LP-partition protocols. The experiments retain failure denominators and distinguish safety-input infeasibility from hard performance-safety conflict.

Section 3 derives the bounded time transformation, adaptive fuzzy nominal controller, robust HOCBF rows, and ITC-FP QP. Section 4 proves the comparison bound, soft-QP pointwise feasibility, conditional safety, and prescribed-time practical tracking. Section 5 reports the numerical protocols and results. ## 2. Problem Formulation and Preliminaries

### 2.1 Two-link Euler–Lagrange model

Consider a planar two-link manipulator with joint position (q=[q_1,q_2]^{\mathsf T}\in\mathbb R^2), joint velocity \(\dot q\), and torque input (u\in\mathbb R^2\). Its physical-time dynamics are

\[
M(q)\ddot q+C(q,\dot q)\dot q+g(q)=u+f(q,\dot q)+d(t),
\tag{1}
\]

where (M(q)) is the inertia matrix, (C(q,\dot q)\dot q) is the Coriolis/centrifugal term, (g(q)) is gravity, (f(q,\dot q)) is an unknown state-dependent matched torque, and (d(t)) is a matched time-varying disturbance. The inertia matrix satisfies

\[
m_-I\preceq M(q)\preceq m_+I,
\qquad 0<m_-\le m_+,
\tag{2}
\]

and the uncertainty bounds are

\[
\|f(q,\dot q)\|\le \bar f,
\qquad \|d(t)\|\le \bar d.
\tag{3}
\]

The numerical model uses

\[
M(q)=
\begin{bmatrix}
2.16+\cos q_2 & 0.58+0.5\cos q_2\\
0.58+0.5\cos q_2 & 0.58
\end{bmatrix},
\tag{4}
\]

\[
C(q,\dot q)=-0.5\sin q_2
\begin{bmatrix}
\dot q_2 & \dot q_1+\dot q_2\\
-\dot q_1 & 0
\end{bmatrix},
\tag{5}
\]

\[
g(q)=9.81
\begin{bmatrix}
1.5\cos q_1+0.5\cos(q_1+q_2)\\
0.5\cos(q_1+q_2)
\end{bmatrix}.
\tag{6}
\]

The end-effector position is

\[
p(q)=
\begin{bmatrix}
\cos q_1+\cos(q_1+q_2)\\
\sin q_1+\sin(q_1+q_2)
\end{bmatrix}.
\tag{7}
\]

The model represents a controlled analytical benchmark. The uncertainty model contains bounded additive matched terms; it does not include unknown input-gain matrices or arbitrary inertia mismatch.

### 2.2 Reference trajectory and tracking objective

The reference moves from (q_0=(0,0)) through (q_v=(-0.3,1.5)) to (q_f=(\pi/2,0)). Let

\[
P(z)=10z^3-15z^4+6z^5.
\tag{8}
\]

For a reference duration \(\tau\), define

\[
q_d(t)=
\begin{cases}
q_0+(q_v-q_0)P(2t/\tau), & 0\le t<\tau/2,\\
q_v+(q_f-q_v)P(2t/\tau-1), & \tau/2\le t<\tau,\\
q_f, & t\ge \tau.
\end{cases}
\tag{9}
\]

The position error is (e=q-q_d). Given a prescribed deadline (t_d) and a tolerance δe>0, the practical tracking objective is

\[
\|e(t)\|\le \delta_e,\qquad t\ge t_d.
\tag{10}
\]

The target is a deadline-defined error tube. The controller is evaluated by deadline accuracy, post-deadline error, full-horizon RMSE, and completion status.

### 2.3 Bounded prescribed-time transformation

Let η denote virtual time and define (a(t)=d\eta/dt>0). The implementable bounded transformation is

\[
a(t)=
\begin{cases}
\big[\alpha(\tau-r)\big]^{-1}, & t<t_s,\\
a_s=(\alpha\varepsilon_\tau)^{-1}, & t\ge t_s,
\end{cases}
\tag{11}
\]

where α>0 and (0<\varepsilon_\tau<\tau). The derivative relations are

\[
\frac{d}{dt}=a\frac{d}{d\eta},
\qquad
\frac{d}{d\eta}=\frac{1}{a}\frac{d}{dt}.
\tag{12}
\]

Freezing the gain at (a_s) avoids a singular physical command and makes the deadline analysis a practical bound. Define the transformed velocity (v=\dot q/a). The reference derivatives in virtual time are

\[
Q_d'=\frac{\dot q_d}{a},
\qquad
Q_d''=\frac{\ddot q_d}{a^2}-\frac{\dot a}{a^3}\dot q_d.
\tag{13}
\]

### 2.4 Safety constraints and HOCBF preliminaries

For obstacle (i), let (o_i\in\mathbb R^2) be its center and (R_i>0) its exclusion radius. Define

\[
h_i(q)=\|p(q)-o_i\|^2-R_i^2.
\tag{14}
\]

The safe set is (\mathcal C_i=\{q:h_i(q)\ge0\}). Since (u) appears in the second derivative of (h_i), the constraint has relative degree two. Define

\[
\psi_{i,0}=h_i,
\qquad
\psi_{i,1}=\dot h_i+c_{i1}h_i,
\tag{15}
\]

with (c_{i1}>0) and (c_{i2}>0). The HOCBF condition is

\[
\dot\psi_{i,1}+c_{i2}\psi_{i,1}\ge0.
\tag{16}
\]

After substituting (1), bounding the uncertainty residual, and collecting the terms affine in (u), (16) becomes

\[
A_i(q,\dot q,t)u\ge B_i(q,\dot q,t).
\tag{17}
\]

The safety function protects the end-effector point. It gives a point-level clearance certificate for (p(q)) and does not represent a whole-link collision certificate.

### 2.5 Input set, safety-input set, and control objectives

The actuator box is

\[
\mathcal U=\{u\in\mathbb R^2:-\bar u_j\le u_j\le\bar u_j,\ j=1,2\}.
\tag{18}
\]

Collecting the HOCBF rows gives

\[
\mathcal S_{\mathrm{HOCBF}}(x,t)=\{u:A_h(x,t)u\ge B_h(x,t)\},
\tag{19}
\]

and the pointwise safety-input set

\[
\mathcal F_s(x,t)=\mathcal U\cap\mathcal S_{\mathrm{HOCBF}}(x,t).
\tag{20}
\]

The control objectives are:

1. achieve the practical tracking bound (10) after the prescribed deadline;
2. maintain (h_i(q)\ge0) and ψi,1\ge0 for all active obstacles;
3. satisfy the torque box (18);
4. quantify the pointwise feasibility of the safety and performance constraints;
5. measure the performance cost introduced by safety filtering.

The condition \(\mathcal F_s(x,t)\ne\varnothing\) is pointwise. It establishes the existence of a safe torque at the current state; persistent feasibility over a time interval requires a separate assumption or audit.

### 2.6 Standing assumptions and useful identities

The analysis assumes a twice continuously differentiable reference with bounded position, velocity, and acceleration; locally regular (M,C,g); bounded (f) and (d); real-state feedback in the continuous-time theorem; initial extended safety (h_i(q_0)\ge0) and ψi,1(q0,q̇ 0)\ge0; and a closed-loop solution that exists in the working domain. The safety-input set remains nonempty on the interval covered by the conditional theorem.

The standard Euler–Lagrange skew-symmetry identity gives

\[
\left(\frac12s^{\mathsf T}Ms\right)'=s^{\mathsf T}(Ms'+Cs),
\tag{21}
\]

where the prime denotes differentiation with respect to η. This identity is the basis of the energy comparison in Section 4.

## 3. ITC-FP Controller and Safety–Performance Coordination

### 3.1 Transformed dynamics

Using (v=\dot q/a), define the physical torque map

\[
u=a^2U+\frac{\dot a}{a}M(q)\dot q+(1-a^2)g(q).
\tag{22}
\]

The transformed dynamics become

\[
Mv'+C(q,v)v+g=U-F+d_\eta,
\tag{23}
\]

where

\[
F(q,v,a)=-\frac{f(q,av)}{a^2},
\qquad
d_\eta=\frac{d(t)}{a^2}.
\tag{24}
\]

The transformation places the deadline scaling in the virtual dynamics while keeping the physical torque finite through the bounded gain (11).

### 3.2 Moving reference and filtered error

Let Λ=ΛT≻0 and define

\[
v_r=Q_d'-\Lambda e,
\qquad
s=v-v_r.
\tag{25}
\]

Since (v=Q') and (e'=Q'-Q_d'),

\[
s=e'+\Lambda e,
\qquad
e'=s-\Lambda e.
\tag{26}
\]

The filtered error (s) is the variable directly dissipated by the virtual controller. The position error follows from the stable first-order filter in (26).

### 3.3 Adaptive fuzzy compensation and robust term

Let (z=[q^{\mathsf T},v^{\mathsf T},a]^{\mathsf T}) be the fuzzy input and Φ(z) its basis vector. On the working domain, approximate the transformed uncertainty as

\[
F(z)=\Theta^{\mathsf T}\Phi(z)+\varepsilon_F(z),
\qquad
\|\varepsilon_F(z)\|\le \bar\varepsilon_F.
\tag{27}
\]

The estimate is (\hat F=\hat\Theta^{\mathsf T}\Phi(z)). The nominal virtual input is

\[
U_0=Mv_r'+C(q,v)v_r+g+\hat F-Ks
-\hat\rho\frac{s}{\sqrt{\|s\|^2+\varepsilon_s^2}},
\tag{28}
\]

where (K=K^{\mathsf T}\succ0), εs>0, and ρ̂ is an adaptive robust gain. The first two terms reproduce the moving reference dynamics, (Ks) supplies quadratic dissipation, and the smoothed robust term absorbs the bounded residual.

The parameter update uses virtual-time adaptation and a projection operator. With positive definite Γ and leakage σΘ>0, a representative form is

\[
\hat\Theta'=-\Gamma\Phi(z)s^{\mathsf T}-\sigma_\Theta\hat\Theta,
\tag{29}
\]

followed by projection onto a compact parameter set (\Omega_\Theta). The robust-gain update has the form

\[
\hat\rho'=\gamma_\rho\|s\|-\sigma_\rho\hat\rho,
\tag{30}
\]

with projection onto ([0,\rho_{\max}]). Projection preserves the estimator bounds. It does not require parameter identifiability for the boundedness result.

### 3.4 Nominal physical torque

The nominal physical torque is obtained from (22):

\[
u_{\mathrm{nom}}=a^2U_0+\frac{\dot a}{a}M(q)\dot q+(1-a^2)g(q).
\tag{31}
\]

The QP selects the applied torque (u) near (u_{\mathrm{nom}}). Define the virtual correction

\[
\Delta U=\frac{u-u_{\mathrm{nom}}}{a^2}.
\tag{32}
\]

This correction is the quantity that enters the transformed energy derivative.

### 3.5 Hard safety rows

For all active obstacles, the HOCBF rows (17) and the actuator box (18) remain hard constraints. The safe-input set is therefore the feasible set of the safety filter:

\[
u\in\mathcal F_s(x,t).
\tag{33}
\]

The safety filter can modify the nominal torque whenever the nominal command violates an HOCBF row or a torque bound. The modification is recorded as an input deviation and is accounted for in the performance analysis.

### 3.6 Hard-performance controller

The hard-performance comparator imposes

\[
s^{\mathsf T}(u-u_{\mathrm{nom}})
\le a^2\big(\chi\|s\|^2+b_{\mathrm{perf}}\big),
\tag{34}
\]

where (0\le\chi<\lambda_{\min}(K)) and (b_{\mathrm{perf}}\ge0). Together with (33), this row defines the Full and ITC-Hard comparisons. If the safe-input set is nonempty but (34) excludes every safe torque, the QP is jointly infeasible.

### 3.7 ITC-FP performance coordination row

ITC-FP retains the energy interpretation of (34) while allowing a nonnegative performance slack:

\[
s^{\mathsf T}(u-u_{\mathrm{nom}})
\le a^2(t)\big(\chi\|s\|^2+b_{\mathrm{perf}}\big)+\delta_p,
\qquad \delta_p\ge0.
\tag{35}
\]

The QP is

\[
\begin{aligned}
\min_{u,\delta_p}\quad
&\frac12(u-u_{\mathrm{nom}})^{\mathsf T}R(u-u_{\mathrm{nom}})+w_p\delta_p^2\\
\mathrm{s.t.}\quad
&u\in\mathcal U,\\
&A_h(x,t)u\ge B_h(x,t),\\
&s^{\mathsf T}(u-u_{\mathrm{nom}})\le a^2(\chi\|s\|^2+b_{\mathrm{perf}})+\delta_p,\\
&\delta_p\ge0,
\end{aligned}
\tag{36}
\]

where (R\succ0) and (w_p>0). The safety rows and torque bounds remain hard. The objective minimizes the local torque deviation and the performance slack; it does not define a global trajectory-optimal controller.

### 3.8 Minimum slack and pointwise feasibility

Define

\[
g_p(u;x,t)=s^{\mathsf T}(u-u_{\mathrm{nom}})-a^2\chi\|s\|^2.
\tag{37}
\]

The minimum slack required by the safe-input set is

\[
\delta_{\mathrm{req}}(x,t)=
\left[
\min_{u\in\mathcal F_s(x,t)}g_p(u;x,t)-a^2b_{\mathrm{perf}}
\right]_+.
\tag{38}
\]

This quantity separates two failure classes:

\[
\begin{array}{ll}
\mathcal F_s=\varnothing &\Rightarrow\texttt{safety\_input\_empty},\\
\mathcal F_s\ne\varnothing, \delta_{\mathrm{req}}>0
&\Rightarrow\texttt{performance\_safety\_conflict}.
\end{array}
\tag{39}
\]

If the safety-input set is nonempty and the slack has no finite upper bound, (36) has a feasible point. If a prescribed upper bound \(\bar\delta_p\) is imposed, pointwise feasibility is equivalent to \(\delta_{\mathrm{req}}\le\bar\delta_p\). This is a current-state criterion; persistent feasibility requires a trajectory-level condition.

### 3.9 Online execution sequence

At each control update, the implementation executes the following steps:

1. evaluate (q,\dot q), the reference, and obstacle geometry;
2. calculate (a,\dot a,Q_d',Q_d'',v_r,s);
3. evaluate the fuzzy estimate and robust term;
4. compute (U_0) and (u_{\mathrm{nom}});
5. assemble the torque box and HOCBF rows;
6. solve (36);
7. apply (u), update the projected adaptive variables, and record δp, (r_p=\delta_p/a^2), constraint residuals, feasibility labels, and performance metrics.

The registered implementation also records the first failure time, safety margin, hard-performance margin, and the quantities required for the LP feasibility partition.

## 4. Stability, Safety, and Feasibility Analysis

### 4.1 Energy inequality

Let

\[
W=\frac12s^{\mathsf T}M(q)s.
\tag{40}
\]

Using (21), the transformed dynamics (23), the nominal controller (28), the bounded approximation residual, and the robust term gives

\[
W'\le -k_s\|s\|^2+B(t)\|s\|+b_{\mathrm{perf}}+r_p(\eta),
\tag{41}
\]

where

\[
k_s=\lambda_{\min}(K)-\chi>0,
\qquad
r_p(\eta)=\frac{\delta_p(t(\eta))}{a^2(t(\eta))}.
\tag{42}
\]

The term (B(t)) collects the bounded reference, disturbance, approximation, smoothing, and time-transformation terms. Applying Young's inequality yields

\[
W'\le -\frac{k_s}{2}\|s\|^2+\frac{B^2(t)}{2k_s}+b_{\mathrm{perf}}+r_p(\eta).
\tag{43}
\]

Because (W\le m_+\|s\|^2/2), define

\[
\kappa=\frac{k_s}{m_+},
\qquad
J_0(\eta)=\frac{B^2(t(\eta))}{2k_s}+b_{\mathrm{perf}}.
\tag{44}
\]

Then

\[
W'\le-\kappa W+J_0(\eta)+r_p(\eta).
\tag{45}
\]

Equation (45) is the central energy relation. It places the safety-induced performance slack inside the same comparison system as the nominal uncertainty and disturbance budget.

### 4.2 Nominal and performance-augmented comparison systems

Define (Z_0) and (Z_p) by

\[
Z_0'=-\kappa Z_0+J_0(\eta),
\qquad
Z_p'=-\kappa Z_p+J_0(\eta)+r_p(\eta),
\tag{46}
\]

with (Z_0(0)=Z_p(0)\ge W(0)). The comparison principle gives (W(\eta)\le Z_p(\eta)). Their difference

\[
D_p=Z_p-Z_0
\tag{47}
\]

satisfies

\[
D_p'=-\kappa D_p+r_p,
\qquad D_p(0)=0,
\tag{48}
\]

and therefore

\[
D_p(\eta)=\int_0^\eta e^{-\kappa(\eta-z)}r_p(z)\,dz.
\tag{49}
\]

The quantity (D_p) is the virtual-time energy increment caused by the relaxed performance row. It is zero when the performance row is satisfied without slack.

### 4.3 Error bound and performance cost

From (e'=s-\Lambda e), let λ=min eig(Λ)>0 and define comparison envelopes

\[
E_p'=-\lambda E_p+\sqrt{\frac{2Z_p}{m_-}},
\qquad
E_0'=-\lambda E_0+\sqrt{\frac{2Z_0}{m_-}}.
\tag{50}
\]

The nominal envelope produces δbase. Since

\[
\sqrt{Z_0+D_p}-\sqrt{Z_0}\le\sqrt{D_p},
\tag{51}
\]

the additional error induced by safety coordination is bounded by

\[
\Delta_{\mathrm{perf}}(\eta)=
\int_0^\eta e^{-\lambda(\eta-z)}
\sqrt{\frac{2D_p(z)}{m_-}}\,dz.
\tag{52}
\]

Consequently,

\[
\|e(t)\|\le\delta_{\mathrm{base}}(t)+
\Delta_{\mathrm{perf}}(\eta(t)).
\tag{53}
\]

If (0\le r_p(\eta)\le\bar r_p), then (D_p\le\bar r_p/\kappa) and

\[
\Delta_{\mathrm{perf}}(\eta)
\le\overline\Delta_{\mathrm{perf}}
 :=\frac1\lambda\sqrt{\frac{2\bar r_p}{m_-\kappa}}.
\tag{54}
\]

When a pointwise uniform bound is unavailable, the controller records (r_p) and evaluates the two integrals (49) and (52) directly. This preserves the measured performance cost without replacing it by an unverified constant.

### 4.4 Pointwise soft-QP feasibility proposition

**Proposition 1 (pointwise feasibility).** Suppose \\mathcal F_s(x,t)≠∅, the functions in (37) are continuous, and δp has no finite upper bound. Then the ITC-FP constraints admit a feasible point.

**Proof.** Choose any (u_s\in\mathcal F_s(x,t)). The value (g_p(u_s;x,t)-a^2b_{\mathrm{perf}}) is finite. Selecting

\[
\delta_p\ge\left[g_p(u_s;x,t)-a^2b_{\mathrm{perf}}\right]_+
\tag{55}
\]

satisfies the performance row while preserving the hard safety and input rows. Hence the feasible set is nonempty. If δp is bounded above by \(\bar\delta_p\), feasibility requires and is guaranteed by \(\delta_{\mathrm{req}}(x,t)\le\bar\delta_p\). □

The proposition identifies the mechanism measured by (38): a nonempty safety-input set can be made compatible with the performance row through a finite, explicitly recorded slack. It does not provide persistent trajectory feasibility by itself.

### 4.5 HOCBF safety theorem

**Theorem 1 (conditional end-effector safety).** Suppose (h_i(q_0)\ge0), ψi,1(q0,q̇ 0)≥0, the closed-loop solution is regular on ([0,T_H]), and every applied torque satisfies the HOCBF row (17). Then ψi,1(t)≥0 and (h_i(q(t))\ge0) on ([0,T_H]).

**Proof.** From (16), the scalar comparison solution for ψi,1 is nonnegative when its initial value is nonnegative. Substituting ψi,1=ẋi+c_i1hi into the first-order inequality gives a second scalar comparison for hi. The initial condition hi(q0)≥0 then implies hi(q(t))≥0. The QP imposes the torque box independently, so the same interval satisfies the input bounds. □

### 4.6 Conditional prescribed-time practical tracking theorem

**Theorem 2 (conditional tracking and safety).** Assume:

1. the model, reference, and uncertainty bounds in Sections 2.1–2.3 hold on a compact working domain;
2. the initial extended safety conditions hold;
3. the exact QP solution is applied and the closed-loop solution remains in the domain;
4. \\mathcal F_s(x,t) remains nonempty on ([0,T_H]);
5. (K\succ0), (0\le\chi<\lambda_{\min}(K)), and (r_p) is measurable with finite exponentially weighted integral.

Then ITC-FP satisfies the torque box and HOCBF constraints on ([0,T_H]), and the tracking error obeys (53). If the right-hand side of (53) is no greater than δe for (t\ge t_d), the manipulator achieves prescribed-time practical tracking.

**Proof.** The hard constraints in (36) give the input and HOCBF inequalities. Theorem 1 gives end-effector safety. Equations (41)–(45) yield the energy comparison (46). Equations (48)–(53) propagate the performance slack into the position-error bound. The deadline statement follows when the computed bound enters the prescribed tolerance tube. □

The theorem states the conditions needed for the result to be used correctly: persistent safety-input feasibility, real-state feedback, exact QP execution, initial extended safety, and a valid working domain. These conditions are tested or diagnosed separately in Section 5.

### 4.7 Design interpretation

The parameter χ allocates a portion of the nominal dissipation to the performance row. Increasing χ enlarges the performance half-space but reduces (k_s). Increasing the allowed slack improves compatibility and increases Δperf according to (49)–(54). Decreasing ετ increases the pre-deadline time gain and can reduce the available torque margin. The QP objective controls the local deviation from (u_{\mathrm{nom}}); it does not establish global path optimality.

## 5. Numerical Experiments and Discussion

### 5.1 Common simulation setting

All experiments use the analytical two-link model (4)–(6), the end-effector map (7), the moving reference (9), the same obstacle geometry, the same torque box, and the same uncertainty-generation protocol. The controller set contains:

- **Safety-only:** actuator and HOCBF constraints without the hard performance row;

### 5.7 Front-obstacle screening and geometric scope

The front-obstacle screen contains 320 tasks. Seventy-nine tasks complete and 241 tasks terminate with an empty safety-input set. These failures define the boundary of the safe-input domain and are reported separately from performance-safety conflicts. Performance slack cannot repair an empty safety-input set because the HOCBF and actuator constraints remain hard.

The safety function in (14) constrains the end-effector point. A whole-link collision certificate requires additional link geometry, multiple barrier functions, or a whole-body safety construction. The current results therefore describe end-effector safety under the stated geometry.

### 5.8 Sampled-data, noise, and delay sensitivity

The sensitivity protocol evaluates several sample-and-hold levels, measurement noise, and a 1 ms execution delay. In the noiseless, zero-delay tests, ITC-FP retains 9/9 completion in each tested implementation level, while Full and ITC-Hard complete 5/9, 5/9, and 4/9 across the tested step sizes. With measurement noise of (10^{-4}) rad and (10^{-3}) rad/s, a 1 ms delay, or both together, ITC-FP still completes 9/9 tasks in the tested batch, but 6/9 tasks show a residual violation in the continuously formulated HOCBF row when evaluated using the actually applied input.

This result identifies the digital implementation requirement. The continuous-time HOCBF theorem is applied to the exact feedback law and does not automatically certify a zero-order-held command under measurement noise and delay. Sampled-data tightening, prediction, or a delay-aware barrier construction is required for a stronger implementation guarantee.

Figure 5-8. Sample-and-hold, noise, and delay sensitivity. Counts use nine tasks per level. Applied-input residuals under noise or delay are discrete implementation evidence and are not a continuous-time hard-safety proof.


### 5.9 Discussion

The experiments answer four questions. First, does ITC-FP work? Under the registered common protocol, it completes every fair holdout task and every task in the 720-task H2 budget row. Second, what explains the improvement? The LP audit and H2 mechanism trace show that the hard performance row removes safe inputs, while the soft performance row retains the safe-input set and records the required slack. Third, can alternative explanations be separated? The audit distinguishes empty safety sets, hard performance conflicts, raw-baseline inapplicability, and solver outcomes. Fourth, where does the method apply? The result applies on the working domain where the safe-input set is nonempty, the feedback and QP conditions hold, and the sampled implementation retains sufficient margin.

The performance cost is part of the result. The normalized slack (r_p), energy increment (D_p), and error increment Δperf provide a direct record of how safety coordination changes the tracking bound. The method therefore provides a compatibility mechanism with an explicit cost, not a universal improvement of all metrics.

## 6. Conclusions and Future Work

This paper developed ITC-FP for prescribed-time practical tracking of an input-constrained two-link manipulator with end-effector obstacle constraints and bounded matched uncertainty. The controller combines a bounded time transformation, a moving-reference filtered error, adaptive fuzzy compensation, a robust HOCBF safety filter, and a QP with hard safety/input rows and a relaxed performance row.

The analysis establishes the central performance-cost chain

\[
\delta_p
\longrightarrow r_p=\frac{\delta_p}{a^2}
\longrightarrow D_p
\longrightarrow \Delta_{\mathrm{perf}},
\tag{57}
\]

and uses it to express the tracking effect of safety filtering in the prescribed-time error bound. The pointwise feasibility proposition separates an empty safety-input set from a hard performance-safety conflict. The conditional theorem combines this feasibility structure with the Lyapunov comparison and HOCBF invariance argument.

The registered numerical evidence shows that ITC-FP improves complete-task feasibility under the common task protocol. It completes 60/60 fair holdout tasks and all 720 budget tasks in H1–H3, whereas the hard-performance controllers fail on 27 fair tasks and all 60 H2 tasks. The 1340-record LP audit confirms the distinction between safety-set emptiness and hard performance conflict. The tuned CT-PD-Safe baseline is reported separately using (k_p=160,k_d=40) and a 60/60 final evaluation.

The next steps are direct extensions of the current model: whole-link collision geometry, discrete-time HOCBF tightening, delay-aware safety constraints, explicit treatment of measurement noise, and hardware experiments with certified execution margins. These extensions preserve the paper's main structure: safety and actuator feasibility remain primary constraints, while performance coordination is quantified through an auditable slack-to-error bound.
